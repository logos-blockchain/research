Total Beer
Inference

One house rule: one beer every thirty seconds. The hard part is that nobody knows how much beer is left in the room.

Filed under Total Stake Inference. Here the stake is beer.

The drinking game is a framing device, not a joke that has to be sustained — drop it the moment it stops earning its keep. Underneath, this is a parameter-selection study from a simulator that models every node separately, at up to 32 000 nodes.

The technical names sit on every slide in small grey type, so anyone who knows the material can map each beer back to the spec.

The rules

Every second, everyone rolls

hit

The more beer you bring, the more dice you roll. About one roll in thirty is a hit, and whoever hits drinks a beer. One at a time, and the barman keeps the tally.

Your beer is your stake. Drinking one is a block. A roll is a slot. The tally is the chain.

Formally: slots are one second, the target rate is f = 1/30, and a node's per-slot win chance is φ = 1 − (1−f)^(w_i/D̂) where w_i is its stake and D̂ the estimate of total active stake.

The one-in-thirty pace is a design choice, and it is the whole talk in miniature: it has to be slow enough that word of a beer gets round the table before the next one is opened.

The problem

Nobody knows how much beer is left

To hold the pace at one beer every thirty seconds, the odds have to match the beer in the room. But people drift off, and take their beer with them.

The beer still in the room is the total active stake. Working it out is the whole problem.

Emphasise still in the room. Not every beer ever bought — the beer that is here and being drunk right now. That is why it cannot be read off the ledger and has to be inferred.

Three things have to hold at once: everyone agrees on the number, the number is right, and nobody profits from bending it. Most of this is the second.

The idea

So the barman watches the tally, and adjusts

Beers going down too fast? More beer in the room than we thought — make the roll harder. Too slow? Make it easier.

# once a week, at every table
m      = rolls that produced a beer
D̂_next = max(1, D̂ · (1 − β·(f − m/T)/f))

The same rule, written out. T is how many rolls it looks back over.

β = 1 in the recommendation, so it fully corrects in one epoch — about a week. Any dent an attacker puts in the number is gone the following week.

It counts a stretch of the night buried deep in settled history, closing well before the number is used. That is why every node computes the same value.

What goes wrong

Only one can drink at a time

That is the rule — but the room is loud. Two people can drink a beer at the same moment without seeing each other, and only one of the two reaches the tally. The other beer is opened, drunk, and never written down.

A beer that misses the tally is an orphan.

Under the deployment's mix-network transport, roughly a third of honest blocks are lost exactly this way. Nobody misbehaved — the room was just louder than the pace of the game.

The precise mechanism, if anyone asks: two leaders build on the same parent because neither has seen the other's block yet. Only one branch survives the fork-choice rule, and the losing block's slot disappears from the count entirely.

What goes wrong

So the tally comes up short

Those unwritten beers never make the count, so the barman thinks less is being drunk than really is — and concludes people have gone home with their beer.

And it feeds itself. A low count makes the roll easier, so more people drink at once, so more beers go unwritten.

0.64 counted, against the beer really drunk

The measured range is 0.64 to 0.74, deepening with the size of the room: 0.739 at a thousand players down to 0.635 at ten thousand, because word takes longer to cross.

In human terms: the barman believes about a third of the table has left, when everyone is still there — and is pouring at up to twice the intended rate as a result.

One clean property: the error only ever goes one way. A stretch of the night cannot contain more drinking rounds than actually happened, so the count can never come out too high.

The fix

Vouch for the beer you saw

When your turn comes you may also vouch for a beer that missed the tally — you saw it drunk, and here is the proof. It goes on the count after all.

Vouching for one is an uncle reference.

Two things have to be decided about that vouch, and they are the two settings this talk is about: how far back you may reach, and how many you may vouch for at once.

Structurally important: a vouch is counting-only. It carries no weight in deciding whose tally is the real one, and a turn is never invalid for leaving a vouch undone.

One limit if asked: only the first beer of a missed run can be vouched for, because anything behind it cannot be checked from the surviving tally alone. Ordinary noise misses single beers, so ordinary recovery is complete.

Does it work?

1.000 counted, against the beer really drunk

One vouch per turn is enough. A second is spare capacity, not accuracy.

The three lines fan out on the left — the louder the room, the deeper the under-count — and collapse onto each other as soon as one vouch is allowed.

What the noise costs

The louder the room, the worse the count

And the vouches hold it exact the whole way.

The clearest single picture of the whole argument: noise is what breaks the count, and one vouch per turn removes the dependence on noise entirely across the design band.

The lines come in pairs because each budget was run under two versions of the counting rule — the one the spec deploys, which can only reach the first beer of a missed run, and an unrestricted ideal. They land on top of each other, which is the finding.

The opposite mistake

Two beers, one round

When both beers do reach the tally, that round still counts once — not twice. Otherwise a lively round looks like more beer in the room, and the barman over-pours instead.

1.017counting beers
1.000counting rounds

Deduplicate per occupied slot, not per block.

Draw the contrast with the earlier slide explicitly: miss a beer and you under-count; record the same round twice and you over-count. The rule threads between them by counting rounds, not beers.

The multi-winner factor is c(f) ≈ 1.017 — a real bias, not a simulation artefact, and one of the two biggest error terms in the deployed estimator. The old "the count can sit above the truth" result people may remember was this bug.

What actually changes

You bring their bottle, not your word

A vouch is not your say-so that someone drank. It is their bottle, with their name on it, set down on the bar — so anyone can check without going to find them.

The header gained no field. body_root replaced block_root and now covers both lists.

This is the block-construction change the uncle work actually requires, spec revision 1.2.0.

The header is still 297 bytes and holds no uncle data of its own. The old block_root, which committed only to the transactions, became body_root, which commits to the serialized uncle_headers list as well. The block ID is the hash of the header, so it transitively commits to the carried entries and their signatures.

Each entry is 361 bytes — a 297-byte header plus its 64-byte signature — and the list carries a one-byte count, so at most 1 + 4 × 361 = 1 445 bytes are added to a 2 MiB body.

Why the whole bottle rather than a note: validity is checked from the referencing chain and the carried entry alone. Availability is structural — vouching for a beer is inseparable from publishing the evidence.

The one number that matters

How many beers overlap?

Word of a beer takes time to get round the table. However many more are opened in the meantime is how many can collide — and each vouch picks up one of them.

3
8
30
0.57 overlapping beers

The report calls this the load, ρ = f · D_vis.

Drag the delay up until the number crosses 1 and let the room watch the verdict change. That crossing is simultaneously the accuracy boundary, the cheating boundary and the history-rewrite boundary.

Formally ρ = f · D_vis with D_vis = hops·δ_max/2 + (hops+1)·ℓ_mean. Mean path latency is held at 1.2 seconds here. In a real deployment you measure it, and how densely nodes peer is the operator-side lever on it.

Setting one

One more vouch than overlapping beers

Any fewer and the backlog never clears — nothing else picks it up.

Round up, then add one: U = ⌈ρ⌉ + 1. Two, at this table.

Read the orange line: one vouch holds the count exact while the overlap is comfortably below one, then peels away exactly where it stops clearing the backlog. That peel-off point is the rule.

Why the spare: at an overlap of 0.96 a single vouch lands at 0.962 ± 0.006, three standard errors short. The tight minimum starts under-shooting from about 0.9.

The spec permits up to four. This table needs two; the spare two are headroom for a louder room.

Setting two

You may vouch twelve rounds back

Counted in rounds, not in seconds. It barely moves when the room gets louder.

Twelve block-intervals — W = 12, or 360 slots, in the spec. The measured floor is about seven.

If this were set by noise, the yellow-to-blue boundary would slope. It does not, which is the whole argument on this slide.

The reach is a buffer for the randomness in when beers land, not for delay. The spec sets W = 12 block-intervals, 360 slots, and states no lower bound; the measured floor is about seven, so twelve carries roughly a 1.7× margin.

The two settings are not interchangeable. No number of vouches buys below the reach floor, and no amount of reach buys below the vouch minimum.

When it breaks · 1

Past one, the night can be rewritten

Below one overlapping beer the tally settles and stays settled. Above it, a group holding a third of the beer can undo as much of the night as it likes.

This is the reorganisation tail at the 99.9th percentile, from a private-chain model. Below the boundary the numbers are small and flat: four blocks against a 10 % adversary, eight against 20 %, seventeen against 30 %. Those are the confirmation depths a wallet would wait.

What happens past the line is not a steeper curve, it is a different regime. Fork-induced orphaning of honest work tips a 30 % holder over an effective majority, and then depth is unbounded — no confirmation depth is safe.

The grey dotted line is honest play with vouching switched off. It climbs too, because the deflated count makes the game over-produce: about seventeen blocks deep at the far right, against four when vouching is on. Vouching keeps ordinary reorganisations shallow as well as keeping the count right.

When it breaks · 2

And past a third, cheating starts to pay

Drink in secret, keep your own tally, and produce it later. Below a third of the beer that loses you money. Above it, it makes money.

Classic selfish mining. Every chain of this design has it — beer did not introduce it.

The three curves are three assumptions about who wins a tie when both tallies are revealed at once. The threshold is (1−γ)/(3−2γ): a third when ties never go the attacker's way, a quarter at even odds, and it falls away to nothing as ties always go theirs.

Two things worth adding. First, this threshold falls as the honest fork rate rises, so keeping the overlap under one is an incentive constraint too, not only an accuracy one — the two breakage slides are the same rule seen twice.

Second, profitability bounds what an attacker earns, not what the count loses. An attacker content to earn what honest play would pay it can push the count down to 0.65 at 40 % stake, where the revenue-maximiser only reaches 0.81. Vouching repairs about half the damage either way.

What TSI does not add: hiding beer to drag the count down is unprofitable on its own, heals within a week, and for a genuine withdrawal the lower number is simply correct.

Where this landed

The spec now says

  • Count rounds, once eacheach slot counted once, however many beers fall in it
  • Vouch twelve rounds backW = 12 → 360 slots
  • Measure the reach from the parentbounds how far back checking has to go
  • Carry up to four vouchesMAX_UNCLES = 4

Still open

  • Two vouches is what this table needs⌈ρ⌉ + 1 — the other two are headroom
  • Pay for vouchesthe spec grants no reward; uncle + nephew under one block reward

Worth saying out loud: the left column is not a wish list. Spec revision 1.2.0 already adopted counting per occupied slot, the parent-anchored window, and W = 12 — three of the things this study argued for.

MAX_UNCLES = 4 is a cap, not an operating point. The overlap at the deployment target is about 0.56, so two is what gets used.

Rewards are the real remaining gap. The spec says an uncle grants no block reward, and that "because uncle references carry no fork-choice weight and grant no reward, a proposer has no incentive to deviate" — a sentence that stops holding the moment they pay.

You can play this

One die each. Six is loud, one is quiet.

Roll a six, take a sip and shout. Roll a one, take a sip and say nothing — and drop a bottle cap in the middle. The Barman has their back turned and ticks a mat when they hear something.

The pile of caps is everything they missed. Turn vouching on and it empties.

0.69five at the table
0.64the simulator

Ticks alone against ticks plus caps. Four players lands at 0.65, six at 0.73 — the same band the study measures.

Rules on one page: beer-game-rules.html, next to this deck.

Fifteen minutes, one die per person, and it reproduces the headline number of the study on a pub table. No arithmetic during play — the Barman only ever counts, and the error is a physical pile rather than a calculation.

Why it works: a round ticks if anyone rolled a six. A one is a real drink that goes unrecorded, but only costs a round when nobody else shouted in it. With five players the Barman ticks 0.598 of rounds while 0.868 actually had a drink — 0.69 of the truth. Four players give 0.65, six give 0.73, and it drifts up towards 0.85 by ten players as shouts start covering the quiet ones.

If someone objects that the mechanism is not identical — they are right. The quiet roll stands in for network delay rather than simulating it. What transfers is the shape: a count that loses events, and a vouch that puts them back.
