Total Stake
Inference

How a blockchain works out how many players are in the game — and what it takes to get that number right.

This deck is pitched so someone with no blockchain background can follow it. The technical names are on the slides in small grey type, so anyone who knows the material can still map every plain word back to the spec.

Scope: a parameter-selection study, from a simulator that models every node separately — its own view of the network, its own chain, its own estimate — at up to 32 000 nodes.

About 20 minutes. Keys: arrows or space to advance, N for notes, T for a dark room, P to export a PDF.

How it works

Every second is a draw

winner

Everyone holds tickets in proportion to the money they have put up. About one draw in thirty produces a winner, and the winner writes the next block.

A block is one entry in the shared record. A draw is a slot.

Formally: slots are one second, the target rate is f = 1/30, and a node's per-slot win chance is φ = 1 − (1−f)^(w_i/D̂) where w_i is its stake and D̂ the estimate of total active stake.

Say "money they have put up" rather than "stake" the first time. The word stake arrives on the next slide, once the mechanism is already clear.

The one-in-thirty rate is a design choice: it has to be slow enough that a block usually reaches everyone before the next one is written. That constraint is the whole talk.

The problem

Nobody knows how many are playing

To hold the rate at one in thirty, the difficulty has to match the tickets in play. But players join and leave without telling anyone.

Their deposits added up are the total active stake. Working it out is the whole problem.

Emphasise active. Not the money that exists — the money that is online and playing right now. That is why it cannot be read off the ledger, and why it has to be inferred.

Three things have to hold at once: every node agrees on the number, the number is right, and nobody profits from bending it. Most of this talk is the second; the third arrives near the end.

Measured: every node computes an identical value — spread ≡ 0 at every size and rate tested. Agreement is not accuracy, though, which is the next few slides.

The idea

So it watches the winners, and corrects itself

Winners showing up too often? More players than we thought — make it harder. Too rarely? Make it easier.

# once a week, at every node
m      = draws that produced a winner
D̂_next = max(1, D̂ · (1 − β·(f − m/T)/f))

The same rule, written out. T is how many draws it looks back over.

β = 1 in the recommendation, so it fully corrects in one epoch — about a week. That means any dent an attacker puts in the number is erased the following week.

Crucially, it counts a window buried deep in settled history, closing well before the number is used. That is why every node computes the same value, and why the churn around the newest blocks never reaches the estimate.

If someone asks why weekly: the window is tied to the security parameter k = 2160, which sets the epoch length. It is a deliberate trade — it means the estimate lags real participation by one to two weeks, and cannot track anything faster.

What goes wrong

News travels slowly

Two people can start writing from the same point, neither knowing about the other. Only one block survives. The other is thrown away — and the draw it won vanishes from the record.

A discarded block is an orphan.

Under the deployment's mix-network transport, roughly a third of honest blocks are orphaned this way. Nobody misbehaved; the network was just slower than the block rate.

The label on the bottom box says "thrown away" rather than "orphan" deliberately — the technical word is on the slide underneath in small type.

What goes wrong

It under-counts the crowd

Those lost draws are missing from the record, so the system counts fewer winners than really happened — and concludes fewer people are playing.

And it feeds itself. A low estimate makes winning easier, so more people write at once, so more blocks get thrown away.

0.64 counted, against what is really there

The measured range is 0.64 to 0.74, deepening with network size — 0.739 at a thousand nodes down to 0.635 at ten thousand, because a bigger network takes longer to cross.

Put the number in human terms: the system believes about a third of the players have gone home, when they are all still there. And it is over-producing blocks at up to twice the intended rate as a result.

One clean property worth stating: the error only ever goes one way. A window cannot contain more winning draws than actually happened, so the estimate can never come out too high. Delay only deflates.

The fix

Let a later block point back

A block can carry a footnote: someone else won that draw too, and here is the proof. The lost draw goes back into the count.

A footnote is an uncle reference.

Two things have to be decided about that footnote, and they are the two parameters this talk is about: how far back it may point, and how many a single block may carry.

Structurally important: footnotes are counting-only. They carry no weight in deciding which chain wins, and a block is never invalid for leaving one out. That comes back as the central design decision near the end.

One limit if asked: only the first block of a discarded branch can be referenced, because anything deeper cannot be checked from the surviving chain alone. Ordinary delay throws away single blocks, so ordinary recovery is complete. A deliberate attack that discards a whole run is only partly recoverable.

Does it work?

1.000 counted, against what is really there

One footnote per block is enough. A second one adds safety margin, not accuracy.

The three lines fan out on the left — the slower the network, the deeper the under-count — and collapse onto each other as soon as one footnote is allowed. That collapse is why the sizing rule later is written in terms of how many blocks are in the air, not in terms of network speed.

A thousand nodes, realistic stake concentration, production security parameter.

What delay costs

The slower the network, the worse the guess

And the footnotes hold it exact the whole way.

This is the clearest single picture of the whole argument: delay is what breaks the count, and one footnote per block removes the dependence on delay entirely across the design band.

The lines come in pairs — solid and dashed — because each footnote budget was run under two versions of the counting rule: the one the spec deploys, which can only reference the first block of a discarded branch, and an unrestricted ideal that could reference any of them. They land on top of each other, which is the finding: inside the design band the restriction costs nothing measurable up to three slots of delay, and about 0.1 % at four and five.

Don't over-read the flat lines. This band is the design regime, where the load stays below one. Push the delay far enough and the footnote budget runs out — that is the next few slides.

Forty paired replicates, each pair sharing the stake draw, the peering graph and every lottery outcome, so the counting rule is the only difference between them.

One detail that decides it

Two winners, one tally

Sometimes two people win the same draw. That draw still counts once — otherwise a lucky second looks like extra players, and the estimate drifts high.

1.017counting blocks
1.000counting draws

Deduplicate per occupied slot, not per block. Change 1 of 3 to the spec.

The multi-winner factor is c(f) ≈ 1.017. This is the smallest change proposed here and one of the two biggest error terms in the deployed estimator.

Worth saying plainly: the old "the estimate can sit above the truth" result people may remember from earlier analysis was this bug, not a property of the estimator. Count draws and the ceiling disappears.

The other residual is change 2 — the target rate is stored on-chain to three decimals, which leaves the number about 1 % high. Measured end to end: 0.99997 at exact f, 1.01026 at the spec's three decimals, 0.99990 at the six recommended.

What actually changes

The footnote is the whole block, carried along

A footnote is not a pointer. It is the discarded block’s entire signed header, carried inside the block — so anyone can check it without hunting for the original.

The header gained no field. body_root replaced block_root and now covers both lists.

This is the block-construction change the uncle work actually requires, spec revision 1.2.0.

The header is still 297 bytes and holds no uncle data of its own. What changed is that the old block_root, which committed only to the transactions, became body_root, which commits to the serialized uncle_headers list as well. The block ID is the hash of the header, so it transitively commits to the carried footnotes and their signatures: two blocks with the same ID are identical byte for byte.

Each entry is 361 bytes — a 297-byte header plus its 64-byte signature. The list is length-prefixed with a single byte, so at most 1 + 4 × 361 = 1 445 bytes are added to a 2 MiB body.

Why carry the whole header rather than a hash: validity is checked from the referencing chain and the carried entry alone. The proof of leadership verifies against the ledger root as of the uncle's parent, which is on the chain. Availability is structural — referencing an uncle is inseparable from publishing its signed header — so no node can be starved of what it needs to decide.

One privacy consequence worth knowing: proposal size now varies with the number of footnotes. The spec preserves indistinguishability at the message layer instead, padding every dispersed payload to a fixed maximum.

The one number that matters

How many blocks are in the air at once?

While one block is still travelling to everyone, others get written. Each footnote rescues one of them — so this number decides how many footnotes a block needs.

8
30
0.57 blocks in the air at once

The report calls this the load, ρ = f · D_vis.

Drag the delay up until the number crosses 1 and let the room watch the verdict change. That crossing is simultaneously the accuracy boundary, the cheating boundary and the history-rewrite boundary — one line doing three jobs.

Formally ρ = f · D_vis with D_vis = hops·δ_max/2 + (hops+1)·ℓ_mean. Mean path latency is held at 1.2 seconds here, the value for the study graph. In a real deployment you measure it, and how densely nodes peer with each other is the operator-side lever on it.

Semi-empirical: the shape of the rule follows from the mechanism, the constants are fitted to the sweeps.

Setting one

One more footnote than there are blocks in the air

Any fewer and the backlog never clears — no other setting rescues it.

Round up, then add one: U = ⌈ρ⌉ + 1. Two, at the deployment target.

Read the orange line: one footnote holds the count exact while the load is comfortably below one, then peels away exactly where it stops clearing the backlog. That peel-off point is the rule.

Why the spare: at a load of 0.96 a single footnote lands at 0.962 ± 0.006, three standard errors short. The tight minimum starts under-shooting from about 0.9, just below each whole number.

The spec already permits up to four. The deployment target needs two, so nothing needs changing — just don't read four as the operating point.

Setting two

A footnote may reach twelve blocks back

Counted in blocks, not in seconds. It barely moves when the network gets slower.

Twelve block-intervals — W = 12, or 360 slots, in the spec. The measured floor is about seven.

If this were set by network speed, the yellow-to-blue boundary would slope. It does not — which is the whole argument on this slide.

The reach is a buffer for the randomness in when blocks appear, not for latency. Near a load of one, widening it to fifteen or twenty block intervals recovers what ten loses — cheaper than buying another footnote slot.

The spec sets W = 12 block-intervals, 360 slots. It states no lower bound, and the measured floor is about seven — so twelve carries roughly a 1.7× margin. That floor is the contribution here. Twelve rather than ten is what the parent-anchored window needs: anchoring on the parent lengthens the gap a footnote has to span, which was measured to parity over 2 112 paired runs.

The two settings are not interchangeable. No number of footnotes buys below the reach floor, and no amount of reach buys below the footnote minimum.

Where this landed

The spec now says

  • Count occupied draws, once eacheach slot counted once, however many blocks fall in it
  • Reach back twelve blocksW = 12 → 360 slots
  • Measure the reach from the parentbounds how far back checking has to go
  • Carry up to four footnotesMAX_UNCLES = 4

Still open

  • Two footnotes is what this network needs⌈ρ⌉ + 1 — the other two are headroom
  • Pay for footnotesthe spec grants no reward; uncle + nephew under one block reward

Worth saying out loud: the left column is not a wish list. Spec revision 1.2.0 already adopted counting per occupied slot, the parent-anchored window, and W = 12 — three of the things this study argued for. The talk is partly a record of why they are right rather than a request to change them.

W = 12 is the parent-anchor figure. Anchoring on the parent lengthens the gap a footnote must span, so the window has to widen by a block-interval or two; twelve was measured to parity over 2 112 paired runs and bracketed to half a block.

MAX_UNCLES = 4 is a cap, not an operating point. The load at the deployment target is about 0.56, so two is what gets used; the spare two are headroom for a slower network.

The load rule is the one thing here with no home in the spec — it constrains the Blend delay budget rather than a consensus constant, so it belongs in deployment guidance.

Rewards are the real remaining gap. The spec says plainly that an uncle grants no block reward, and that "because uncle references carry no fork-choice weight and grant no reward, a proposer has no incentive to deviate" — a sentence that stops holding the moment they pay. Three things would need reconciling: that rationale, the content-independent reward voucher, and the fact that a discarded block never executes, so its author has no payout path at all.
